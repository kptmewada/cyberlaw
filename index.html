Points to verify before you rely on the PPTs

These are places where the PPTs disagree with each other or with current law.

  • Section 66A. Struck down by the Supreme Court in Shreya Singhal v Union of India (24 March 2015). Several PPTs still describe it as operative.
  • Section 43 compensation cap. Cyber Contraventions PPT reprints the old cap of Rs 1 crore. The 2008 amendment removed it, as the Overview PPT text shows.
  • Section 66. Online Privacy PPT describes the old “hacking” version. Cyber Contraventions and Overview describe the current 3 years / Rs 5 lakh version.
  • Sections 67 and 67A. The Cyber Contraventions PPT summary leaves out the higher second-conviction punishment and says “or both” for s.67. Check the bare Act.
  • Section 48 v 46. The Overview PPT footnote labels the tribunal provision “Section 46”. It is Section 48. Section 46 is the power to adjudicate.
  • Cyber Appellate Tribunal. Merged into TDSAT by the Finance Act, 2017.
  • IT Rules 2011. Intermediary Guidelines 2011 replaced by the 2021 Rules (amended 2026). SPDI Rules and s.43A continue until 13 May 2027.
  • IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act. Replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam from 1 July 2024. PPT section numbers are for the old codes.
  • Section 5, digital v electronic signature. E-signature PPT prints the older wording “digital signature”. The Overview PPT has “electronic signatures”.
  • Section 47 factors. The PPT sentence repeats one factor. There are three: unfair advantage, loss caused, and repetition.
  • RAM description in the Scope PPT. Static RAM does not keep data when power is off, and a pen drive is flash memory. Do not reproduce that line as a fact.
  • Syllabus item “NPCA”. Not identified in the PPTs. Confirm the full form with your institute.

Introduction to information technology
  • Computers are used for billing, ATM withdrawals, communication by e-mail, storing data (including cloud storage) and moving money across countries within minutes.
  • Without a computer there is no cyber space, and so no cyber law. A computer is defined in s.2(1)(i); data in s.2(1)(o); function in s.2(1)(u); computer system in s.2(1)(l); computer network in s.2(1)(j).
  • Evolution: Charles Babbage's steam-powered “Difference Machine” (1800s) came first. Then five generations: 1st 1945–1956 (vacuum tubes, room-sized, ballistic charts for the U.S. Navy); 2nd 1956–1963 (transistors); 3rd 1964–1971 (semi-conductor chips, operating systems); 4th 1971 to present (LSI, VLSI, ULSI, networking, internet); 5th present (smart phones, voice recognition and typing).
  • Functioning: hardware and software; input and output devices; CPU; ALU; memory measured in bytes, KB, MB, GB; RAM and ROM; hard disk; motherboard; operating system as the backbone of software.
  • Internet: begun in the 1960s to keep communication alive during missile attack. World Wide Web works on HTTP; TCP/IP and SMTP are the key protocols; an IP address has four octets numbered 0–255; the Domain Name System maps easy names to IP addresses.
Outside the PPTs
  • ARPANET (1969) is the usual starting point of the internet. The Web was proposed by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN around 1989–91.
  • The PPT's RAM description is technically inaccurate. Both static and dynamic RAM are volatile, and a pen drive uses non-volatile flash memory. TCP is Transmission Control Protocol and SMTP is Simple Mail Transfer Protocol. Use the correct names in answers.
Scope of Cyber Law
Meaning, jurisprudence and scope of cyber law
  • Jurisprudence is the philosophy of law. It studies the principles behind law and how law operates in a society.
  • “Cyber” comes from “cybernetics”, from the Greek kubernetes (governor), a term coined by the mathematician Norbert Wiener in the 1940s.
  • Cyber law governs cyber space, meaning the internet and electronic data on a standalone computer. If it covered only the internet, standalone systems would fall outside it.
  • Why needed: a borderless society (a person in London can hack a computer elsewhere); internet banking fraud; loss of traditional jurisdiction and sovereignty; illegal interception; exposure of children to pornography.
  • Examples of cyber-law matters: unauthorised access to e-mail; violating a registered trade mark through a domain name.

The IT Act, 2000: genesis, objectives and structure
  • Origin: the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce (1996), adopted by UN General Assembly resolution A/RES/51/162 of 30 January 1997. The Bill of 1999 was passed in May 2000, received assent on 9 June 2000 and came into force on 17 October 2000.
  • Structure as stated in the PPT: 13 Chapters and 90 Sections. Its main heads are legal recognition of electronic records and signatures, e-governance, regulation of Certifying Authorities, penalties and adjudication, offences, and the Cyber Appellate Tribunal.
  • Extent: the whole of India, and offences or contraventions outside India involving a computer located in India (s.1(2), 75). The First Schedule exclusions are in s.1(4), First Schedule.
  • Consequential amendments were made to the Indian Penal Code, the Indian Evidence Act, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act and the Reserve Bank of India Act.

Major amendments: IT (Amendment) Act, 2008 and suggested reforms

Basics of e-commerce
  • E-commerce is the basis of electronic contracting. The UNCITRAL Model Law on E-Commerce (MLEC), 1996 rests on three principles: non-discrimination, technology neutrality and functional equivalence.
  • MLEC articles relied on: Art. 5 (legal recognition of data messages), Art. 6 (writing), Art. 7 (signature), Art. 8 (original), Art. 10 (retention) and Art. 11 (formation and validity of contracts).
  • Indian counterparts: s.4 (writing), s.5 (signature), s.7 (retention), s.10A (contracts), s.11 to s.13 (attribution, acknowledgement, time and place).
  • The Indian Contract Act, 1872 does not define electronic contracts. Its ss. ICA s.10, ICA s.11 and ICA s.23 still apply.

  • Models: B2B, B2C, C2C and B2G. M-commerce is e-commerce over mobile devices.
  • Consumer side: the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 set duties for marketplace and seller entities.

Cyber security fundamentals, techniques and core principles
  • Cyber security is defined in the IT Act, and the NCSP 2013 states that protecting the confidentiality, integrity and availability of information in cyberspace is the essence of a secure cyber space.
  • The US PPT defines information security to include integrity, confidentiality and availability, and describes encryption as a protective technique.
  • NCSP 2013 strategies that act as techniques: a CISO in each organisation, information security policies, security audits, ISO 27001 ISMS certification, penetration testing, vulnerability assessment, PKI, secure software development, business continuity and crisis management plans, and drills.
  • Encryption modes and methods may be prescribed by Government (s.84A).
Outside the PPTs
  • Core principles: confidentiality, integrity, availability (the CIA triad), plus authentication, authorisation, non-repudiation (which digital signatures give) and accountability.
  • Design ideas: defence in depth, least privilege, separation of duties, fail-safe defaults.
  • Controls: firewalls, intrusion detection, VPNs, multi-factor authentication, patching, backups, encryption, logging and monitoring.
  • IT Act definition: s.2(1)(nb) “cyber security” means protecting information, equipment, devices, computer, computer resource, communication device and information stored therein from unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification or destruction.

IT Rules 2011
  • SPDI Rules 2011 (under s.43A): a body corporate must adopt a privacy policy, obtain consent for collecting sensitive personal data, limit retention and disclosure, and follow reasonable security practices such as IS/ISO/IEC 27001 (Rule 8). See SPDI Rules 2011.
  • Intermediary Guidelines 2011 (under s.79(2) and s.87(2)(zg)): due diligence an intermediary must observe to keep the exemption. The intermediary must not initiate transmission, select the receiver, or select or modify the transmission.
  • Cyber Cafe Guidelines 2011: registration with a registration agency, user identification records (photocopy or scan of ID), log register, physical layout and inspection.
  • Other rules listed in s.87: CRAT Procedure Rules 2000, Security Procedure Rules 2004, Interception, Blocking and Traffic Data Rules 2009.

Cyber space, technology and issues
  • Cyberspace (NCSP 2013, citing ISO/IEC 27032:2012) is a complex environment of interactions between people, software and services, supported by worldwide ICT devices and networks. Citizens, business, critical infrastructure, military and government all share it, so clear boundaries are hard to draw.
  • Technologies raising legal issues (US PPT): cloud computing (on-demand shared services, also abused to amplify criminal bandwidth); encryption (plaintext turned into ciphertext with an algorithm and key); the Internet of Things (interconnected devices, including implants, and a target for botnets).
  • Threats (NCSP 2013): identity theft, phishing, social engineering, hactivism, cyber terrorism, mobile threats, compromised digital certificates, advanced persistent threats, denial of service, botnets, supply chain attacks, data leakage.
  • Legal issues: borderless acts and jurisdiction; anonymity; privacy (cookies, web bugs, profiling); admissibility of electronic evidence; validity of e-contracts and signatures.

Regulating cyberspace: Budapest Convention (international)
  • First binding international treaty on crimes over the internet and computer networks (Council of Europe, Treaty 185). Supplemented by a Protocol on xenophobia and racism through computer systems. The PPT says 61 nations had signed and entered into force.
  • Aim: a harmonised approach that cuts “safe havens” and helps law-enforcement cooperation.
  • Offences (Chapter II, Arts. 2–10): (1) against confidentiality, integrity and availability of computer data and systems; (2) computer-related fraud and forgery; (3) content offences (child pornography); (4) criminal copyright infringement. Art. 11 attempt and aiding and abetting; Art. 12 corporate liability; Art. 13 sanctions.
  • Omitted: identity theft, grooming of children, spam and cyberterrorism.
  • Procedure (Chapter II, Section 2): expedited preservation of stored data; preservation and partial disclosure of traffic data; production orders; search and seizure of stored data; real-time collection of traffic data; interception of content data.
  • Jurisdiction (Art. 22): see Budapest Art. 22.
  • Co-operation (Chapter III): Art. 23 to the widest extent possible; Art. 24 extradition (Arts. 2–11 offences deemed included in extradition treaties); Arts. 27–34 mutual assistance; Art. 35 a 24/7 contact network.

E-contract and Electronic Data Interchange (EDI)
  • Validity: s.10A says a contract is not unenforceable solely because it is in electronic form. The Contract Act essentials still apply: free consent and lawful object (ICA s.10), capacity (ICA s.11), lawful consideration and object (ICA s.23).
  • EDI: the oldest form of electronic transaction. Parties exchange structured messages in an agreed standard, designed for automatic processing (for example, orders, invoices, waybills). Both sides must use the same messaging standard.
  • Click-wrap: the online version of the shrink-wrap licence. The user clicks “I agree”. US courts upheld such terms (for example Hotmail v Money Pie, CompuServe v Patterson, Register.com v Verio).
  • Browse-wrap: terms such as “By using this site, you agree” with no express assent. Validity depends on whether the user had notice (Specht v Netscape).
  • E-mail contracts: offer and acceptance by e-mail, so the parties can bargain. In Trimex International FZE v Vedanta Aluminium the Supreme Court recognised e-mail exchange as a binding contract.
  • Problems: click-wrap and browse-wrap are take-it-or-leave-it and give no way to check capacity (age, sound mind). An object lawful in one country may be unlawful in another.
  • Formation rules: s.11 (attribution), s.12 (acknowledgement), s.13 (time and place of dispatch and receipt).

E-signature: legality under the IT Act, 2000
  • Digital signature (s.2(1)(p)) authenticates an electronic record using an asymmetric crypto system and hash function (s.3, s.2(1)(f)). The subscriber signs with a private key (s.2(1)(zc), (zd)); anyone verifies with the public key listed in the certificate. The two keys form a key pair (s.2(1)(x)).
  • Electronic signature (s.3A) widens the law to any reliable technique listed in the Second Schedule. Reliability means the signature data is linked only to the signatory, was under the signatory's control, and any later change to the signature or the record is detectable.
  • Legal recognition: s.5 treats authentication by electronic signature as satisfying any law that requires a signature. s.4 does the same for “writing”.
  • Secure electronic signature: s.14, 15 (exclusive control of signatory).
  • Certifying Authorities: licensed under s.24; defined in s.2(1)(g); issue certificates under s.35, 37, 38; may suspend or revoke them (s.35, 37, 38 lists ss. 37, 38). Certificate: s.2(1)(q). Controller of Certifying Authorities: s.17, 18. Subscriber duties: s.40A.
  • Evidence: the Evidence Act provisions IEA s.47A, IEA s.67A, IEA s.73A, IEA s.85A, IEA s.85B, IEA s.85C, IEA s.90A give evidentiary weight and presumptions to digital signatures.
Outside the PPTs
  • The Second Schedule now includes e-authentication using Aadhaar e-KYC (added by notification in 2015), which is the basis of “e-Sign” services.
  • Digital signature v electronic signature: a digital signature is one specific technology (PKI). An electronic signature is technology-neutral and includes digital signatures.
  • Under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 the electronic-evidence provisions have been renumbered.

  • s.6: filing, licences, permits and payments with Government bodies can be done electronically in the form the Government prescribes.

ISPs and websites: legal liability under the IT Act, 2000
  • Who is an intermediary: s.2(1)(w), (ha). ISPs and websites hosting third-party content come within it.
  • Safe harbour (s.79): an intermediary is not liable for third-party information if it only provides access, does not initiate transmission, select the receiver or select or modify the content, and observes due diligence and the Government's guidelines.
  • Loss of safe harbour: if it conspired, abetted, aided or induced the unlawful act, or, after actual knowledge or notification by the appropriate Government, fails to expeditiously remove or disable access without vitiating evidence (s.79(3)).
  • Duties and penalties: preserve and retain information (s.67C); assist interception and decryption (s.69); comply with blocking orders (s.69A); assist traffic-data monitoring (s.69B).
  • Guidelines 2011: due-diligence rules for intermediaries and cyber cafes (see the IT Rules 2011 topic in Unit 1).
  • Cases in the PPTs: Bazee.com (Avnish Bajaj v State, 2008): an obscene clip stayed listed despite notice, so the CEO was proceeded against under s.67 with s.85. Super Cassettes v MySpace: the IT Act does not exclude civil jurisdiction in copyright cases (s.81 proviso).
Updated since the PPT
  • Shreya Singhal v Union of India (2015) read down s.79(3)(b): the platform must act on a court order or a Government notification, not on private complaints.
  • The IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 (amended in 2026) now set due diligence, grievance redressal and takedown timelines, and significant social media intermediaries carry extra duties.
  • MySpace Inc v Super Cassettes Industries (Delhi HC Division Bench, 2016) held that copyright safe harbour needs specific knowledge, such as a notice identifying the infringing URLs, and set aside the wider injunction.

Adjudication process for recovery of losses
  1. Complaint: the affected person claims compensation for a contravention of s.43 or s.43A.
  2. Adjudicating Officer (s.46): not below the rank of Director to the Government of India (or equivalent state officer), with experience in law and IT. Holds an enquiry with civil-court powers (s.46(5)).
  3. Pecuniary limit (s.46(1A)): claims up to Rs 5 crore. Above that, the competent court decides.
  4. Quantum (s.47): unfair advantage gained, loss caused, and repetition of default.
  5. Bar on civil courts (s.61): matters within the adjudicating officer's power cannot be taken to a civil court, subject to s.81 (proviso) and the case law noted in s.61.
  6. Appeal (s.48): the Cyber Appellate Tribunal. Then to the High Court within 60 days on a question of fact or law (s.62).
  7. Residuary penalty: s.45. Rules: CRAT Procedure Rules 2000 (s.87).

IPR and cyber space
  • Copyright: the IT Act does not restrict rights under the Copyright Act, 1957 or the Patents Act, 1970 (s.81 (proviso)). Civil suits for infringement lie in the district court (Copyright Act s.62(1)); Super Cassettes v MySpace shows a plaintiff can sue where he carries on business.   • Trade marks and domain names: the Scope PPT notes that unlawful violation of a registered trade mark through a domain name is a cyber law matter. India TV v India Broadcast Live and Banyan Tree v A. Murali Krishna Reddy are passing-off cases in which jurisdiction over a website was tested.

s.43Penalty and compensation for damage to computer, computer system, etc.

If any person without permission of the owner or any other person who is in charge of a computer, computer system or computer network,—
(a) accesses or secures access to such computer, computer system or computer network or computer resource
(b) downloads, copies or extracts any data, computer data base or information from such computer, computer system or computer network including information or data held or stored in any removable storage medium;
(c) introduces or causes to be introduced any computer contaminant or computer virus into any computer, computer system or computer network;
(d) damages or causes to be damaged any computer, computer system or computer network, data, computer data base or any other programmes residing in such computer, computer system or computer network;
(e) disrupts or causes disruption of any computer, computer system or computer network;
(f) denies or causes the denial of access to any person authorised to access any computer, computer system or computer network by any means;
(g) provides any assistance to any person to facilitate access to a computer, computer system or computer network in contravention of the provisions of this Act, rules or regulations made thereunder;
(h) charges the services availed of by a person to the account of another person by tampering with or manipulating any computer, computer system, or computer network,
(i) destroys, deletes or alters any information residing in a computer resource or diminishes its value or utility or affects it injuriously by any means;

(b) by any means to usurp the normal operation of the computer, computer system, or computer network;
(ii) “computer database” means a representation of information, knowledge, facts, concepts or instructions in text, image, audio, video that are being prepared or have been prepared in a formalised manner or have been produced by a computer, computer system or computer network and are intended for use in a computer, computer system or computer network;
